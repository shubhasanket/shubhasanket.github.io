This project, carried out at ENS Paris-Saclay jointly with **Kshitij Ambilduke** as part of the Multimodal Explainable AI course, studies whether language-guided concept bottlenecks can produce *interpretable-by-design* speech emotion classifiers without giving up too much accuracy. Highly accurate emotion models are usually opaque, while interpretable models are often dismissed as too restrictive — and post-hoc explanations need not faithfully reflect the underlying reasoning.

We build on **LaBo** (*Language in a Bottle*), which uses a large language model to generate a candidate set of natural-language concepts per class, scores their presence in an input via a frozen vision-language model, and predicts the label from those concept activations through a lightweight linear classifier with a language prior on its weights. Concepts are selected from the candidate pool by maximizing a submodular objective that balances class-discriminability against semantic coverage, giving a small, diverse, label-free bottleneck per class.

**Our contribution — adapting LaBo to speech.** We swap the vision-language encoder for **CLAP**, an audio-text contrastive model, and reformulate the concept space around prosody, energy, voice quality, and expressive style — acoustic-emotional descriptors such as *high vocal tension*, *flat intonation*, or *slow low-energy speech*. Concepts are generated by an LLM in a two-stage prompt-then-rerank pipeline, scored against pre-extracted CLAP audio embeddings, and fed to a linear classifier with the LaBo language prior. We evaluate the full bottleneck pipeline on the IEMOCAP corpus against several baselines: a zero-shot CLAP classifier, a k-nearest-neighbour retrieval baseline, a CLAP linear/MLP probe, and an end-to-end CNN trained on log-mel spectrograms.

**What we found.** Zero-shot CLAP performs at chance, k-NN over frozen CLAP audio embeddings recovers a useful emotion signal, and the CLAP Probe and end-to-end CNN reach the strongest accuracy. LaBo sits below k-NN, which is the key diagnostic: routing predictions through the alignment-score bottleneck *removes* discriminative information rather than refining it. Inspecting the learned weight matrix shows that for classes where CLAP alignment is at least partly informative — *angry*, *excited*, *frustrated*, *sad*, *neutral* — the model latches onto acoustically appropriate concepts; on more ambiguous classes such as *fear* and *surprise* the weights are diffuse. The interpretability goal of the framework remains well-motivated, but in the audio setting it will likely require either a stronger audio-text alignment model than CLAP, or a concept vocabulary designed more directly around the structure of CLAP's embedding space.
